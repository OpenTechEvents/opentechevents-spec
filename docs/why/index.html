You already know the problem #

You organise one event. Then you announce it over and over, by hand, across several networks whose algorithms decide who even sees it. In today's attention economy, your message competes against all the noise — and you still miss half the people who would have come.

📋

The same event, announced again and again

Your website, Meetup, LinkedIn, Instagram, X, several WhatsApp and Telegram groups… The same information on platforms where content can easily go unnoticed.

🕳️

Directories can't find you

Event directories want to list you. But they'd have to find your event, or wait for you to submit a form, open a pull request, send an email, and so on… So they almost never do.

🔁

Every change means doing it all over

Venue moved. Speaker cancelled. New date. Now go update every single platform — or live with the stale copies out there.

There is no shortage of information about your events. What's missing is interoperability.

What you get out of it #

Adopting OTE is under an hour of work the first time. After that, a couple of minutes per event you publish. Here's what it buys you.

⏱️

Stop doing data entry

Write the event once. Let tooling push it to directories, calendars and feeds instead of you copy-pasting it into yet another form.

📣

Reach beyond your own bubble

Directories and aggregators will be able to pick up your events the moment you publish them, and reach each person in the channel they already use — not the one you happen to pick. Your meetup can show up where people are actually looking.

🔍

And you show up better in Google

Google and other search engines read schema.org/Event to show event rich results: date, venue and price right there in the search results. OTE wasn't designed for SEO, but it maps onto that vocabulary almost 1:1 — so if you can touch your site's HTML, the same tooling that builds your feed will be able to generate the JSON-LD for you to paste in. One file, four destinations.

🔓

Own your data

Your events live at your URL, in an open format. If a platform shuts down, changes its API or its pricing, your history and your feed are still yours.

🧩

An open, community-owned ecosystem

Because OTE is an open spec owned by the community itself, it has the potential for a whole ecosystem of free, open integrations. Every new tool built on it — an .ics export, a website widget, a Telegram bot — works for you without you lifting a finger.

✅

Accurate everywhere

Change the date in one place and every consumer that re-reads your feed gets the correction. No more zombie listings with stale information.

🌱

Shape the standard

The spec is in draft. Early adopters decide what it looks like — if something doesn't fit your community, it can still be changed.

Not sure it's worth the effort? Read this first.

Keep using what you already have, import it easily, improve it a little and get big benefits.

No JSON to write, no tool to change: your events from Meetup, your .ics, Eventbrite… we help you import them as they are. By adding just a few more fields — topics (tags), attendance mode, language, coordinates — you multiply their reach.

Those are the fields that let someone filter by tags, attendance mode or coordinates; and because it's an open, free standard —and because it isn't a specific platform or one with restricted APIs, but an RSS-style specification designed specifically to solve the problems of tech communities and events—, it's far easier for people to find you and subscribe to what interests them, just as other directories can give your event visibility with no effort.

It is also what you gain going from an .ics to OTE. A VEVENT says when and where, and anything it has no field for — a call for proposals with its own dates, online or in person, who organises it, under what licence, in which language — ends up as prose inside DESCRIPTION, where no tool can filter on it. The event world gets more fragmented and more siloed every year; a feed of well-tagged open data, in a vocabulary built for this domain, is what lets someone find you a year from now — a person looking for exactly your topic, or a company looking for events like yours to support.

Frequently asked questions #

Does this compete with Meetup, Eventbrite or Luma?

No. OTE is a data format, not a platform. The point is to interoperate with those tools: describe the event once, then publish or convert it towards whatever platforms you already use.

Do I have to stop using my current tools?

No, not at all. The goal is for your data to flow easily to many more platforms — and, if you use several, to speed up the work of re-entering it in each one.

Does it replace RSS or iCalendar?

No. OTE is designed to be convertible to them. An OTE feed can be exported to RSS, JSON Feed or iCal so attendees consume it in their feed reader or calendar app. What each of those standards already solves, and what we add on top, is laid out in prior art.

How do attendees subscribe?

However they prefer. Some want events in their calendar, others by email, others in a WhatsApp or Telegram group, others in an RSS reader or their social feeds. Because it's an open specification, each tool in the ecosystem can cover a different preference — no one has to bend to one platform's app. You publish once; each person picks their channel.

How is this different from schema.org/Event?

schema.org/Event isn't a rival to OTE, it's a mapping target: OTE serialises to JSON-LD almost 1:1 and can be exported to schema.org so Google and aggregators like dev.events pick you up. The difference is that schema.org describes one event embedded in one HTML page — there's no notion of a feed: you can't subscribe to «all of a community's events» or consume them without scraping HTML — its vocabulary is huge and almost entirely optional, and it ships no validation. OTE fills those gaps: a subscribable feed (one URL, N events, with an updatedAt) as a plain JSON file, a small validatable core, and online/hybrid and CFP as first-class fields. In one line: OTE = schema.org's data model + RSS/iCal's distribution model, without making you choose. The longer answer — including h-event, ActivityPub and what we can't claim yet — is in prior art.

Does this help my SEO?

Yes, but two things are worth keeping apart. Your OTE feed (events.json) is for directories, calendars and bots: Google won't index it as if it were your page. What Google does read is the schema.org/Event JSON-LD embedded in your event's HTML, and from that it can show rich results with date, venue and price. Because OTE serialises to that vocabulary almost 1:1, the event you've already written is enough to generate that snippet and paste it into your page — so the SEO comes for free, with no hand-written markup to maintain on the side. Two honest caveats: you need your own event page and the ability to edit its HTML, and the schema.org exporter is still proposed, not built (see the tools section).

How much work is this really?

For a small community: writing one JSON file and adding a link tag to your site. If you already generate your events page from data, it's usually a matter of emitting one more file.

Is the spec stable?

Not yet. We're at version 0.x and everything — name, fields, licence, governance — is open to debate. That's precisely why joining now is worth it: early adopters shape the format.

Who is behind this?

The proposal comes from Community Builders (ComBuildersES) and is now developed in its own home, the OpenTechEvents organisation on GitHub, with an international scope. It is not an official standard yet — it's an open, community-driven design effort, and it needs organisers like you to become one.

A standard is worth exactly as much as the number of people who use it #

Every community that publishes an OTE feed makes the format more useful for every other community — and for everyone trying to find your events.
